## Introduction

Every RAG postmortem I've been part of starts the same way: someone proposes swapping the embedding model. It's the visible, nameable piece of the pipeline — you can point at a leaderboard, pick a bigger number, and feel like you did something. In three separate retrieval pipelines I've worked on, that swap produced a rounding error in end-to-end quality. The reranker, which almost nobody wants to spend time on, was where the actual gains lived.

This isn't a contrarian take for its own sake. It's a specific claim about where the leverage is in a retrieval pipeline, and it's worth understanding why.

## What each stage is actually optimizing for

An embedding model has to compress an entire document — arbitrary length, arbitrary topic — into a fixed-size vector, and it has to do this in a way that's useful for *every possible query* someone might later ask. That's an enormous amount of information loss happening under a huge amount of uncertainty about what will matter.

A reranker has a much narrower job: given a specific query and a specific candidate document, score how relevant they are to *each other*. It's not compressing anything into a lossy fixed-size representation ahead of time — it can look at the query and the full document together and make a judgment call with all the information present.

The embedding stage is a triage step working blind. The reranker is a judgment step working with full context. It should not be surprising that the judgment step gets more value from marginal improvement — but the industry's attention is inverted, because embedding models are the part with public benchmarks and version numbers to chase.

## What we measured

On an internal support-ticket retrieval corpus (around 400K documents), we ran four configurations holding the corpus and query set fixed:

| Configuration | Recall@10 |
|---|---|
| Small embedding model, no reranker | 61% |
| Large embedding model, no reranker | 66% |
| Small embedding model + cross-encoder reranker | 89% |
| Large embedding model + cross-encoder reranker | 91% |

Upgrading the embedding model alone bought 5 points. Adding a reranker on top of the *smaller, cheaper* embedding model bought 28 points — and closed almost all of the gap to the large-model-plus-reranker configuration. The reranker wasn't a nice-to-have on top of a good embedding model; it was doing most of the actual work of relevance.

## Why this happens in practice

Dense embeddings are good at capturing *topical* similarity — documents about the same general subject cluster together. They're bad at precision on the details that actually distinguish a correct answer from an almost-correct one: an exact error code, a specific config key, a version number, a negation ("does not support" vs "supports"). These are exactly the details support queries hinge on, and exactly what a fixed-size vector tends to smooth over.

A cross-encoder reranker sees the full query and document text together, at inference time, with no compression step in between. It can notice that a candidate document mentions the wrong version number even though it's topically perfect — something no amount of embedding-model scale reliably fixes, because the problem isn't model capacity, it's the architecture of compressing first and comparing later.

## Where the real trade-off lives

None of this means embedding model choice doesn't matter — it sets your recall ceiling before reranking ever gets a chance to help, and a bad enough first-stage retriever won't put the right document in the candidate set for the reranker to rescue. The practical implication is about where to spend your first optimization pass:

1. Get a reasonable, not necessarily state-of-the-art, embedding model retrieving a wide candidate set (k=50–100)
2. Invest real effort in reranker quality — model choice, calibration against your own query distribution, and latency budget
3. Only then revisit the embedding model, once the reranker's ceiling is the binding constraint

Most teams do this in the opposite order, because the embedding model is the part with a name and a leaderboard rank. The reranker is the part that actually earns its keep.
